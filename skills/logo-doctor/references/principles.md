# What a senior designer checks

The checklist an experienced identity designer runs through when judging or making a mark, with a practical test
for each point. The construction rules (grids, weights, curves, optical corrections) are in `construction.md`;
colour is in `colour.md`.

## 1. The order of a review

Big things first, details last; a perfect curve does not save a mark that says the wrong thing.

1. Fit: does it suit the business, the audience and the market?
2. Idea: is there one clear idea, and does it read in a second?
3. Silhouette: does the overall shape hold when filled solid and blurred?
4. Construction: grid, weights, gaps, curves, angles.
5. Optics: does it look right, not just measure right?
6. Scale: favicon to billboard.
7. Colour: light, dark, one colour, colour-blind viewers, print.
8. Type: spacing, weight match with the mark, legibility.
9. System: versions, clear space, minimum size.
10. Risk: similarity to existing marks, licences.

## 2. Brief and fit

A logo identifies; it does not have to explain the business. It has to feel right for it.

- **Audience and category.** A bank, a toy shop and a security firm need different tones. Check the mark against
  the category: does it look like it belongs, and does it stand apart from the main competitors?
- **Personality.** Ask the owner for three words that describe the brand (for example: precise, calm, senior).
  Then check each visual choice against them: heavy rounded strokes say friendly, thin sharp ones say precise.
- **Literal is not better.** A gear for an engineering firm, a tooth for a dentist: common, forgettable, and hard
  to protect. One clear, ownable idea beats an illustration of the service.

Test: show the mark without the name to someone outside the business and ask what kind of company it might be.
The answer should be in the right family, not necessarily exact.

## 3. The five qualities

Design teachers commonly list five qualities of a strong logo. Each has a quick test.

| Quality | What it means | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple | few elements, one idea | Count the parts. Squint (the blur test in `08-sizes.png`): the idea should survive. |
| Memorable | can be recalled and drawn | Show it for ten seconds, hide it, ask someone to sketch it. Key features should come back. |
| Timeless | not tied to a passing style | Name the trend it uses (gradient, glow, a font of the year). If the mark depends on it, it will date. |
| Versatile | works at every size, in one colour, on any ground | One-colour version, 16 px version, reversed version, embroidered or engraved. |
| Appropriate | fits the business and audience | The fit test in section 2. |

A detailed mark can still be strong, but then it needs a simplified version for small sizes (section 6).

## 4. Space and perception

People do not see shapes one by one; the eye groups and completes them. These Gestalt principles explain most
"it looks off" reactions.

- **Figure and ground.** The space between and inside shapes (counters) is part of the design. Check the negative
  shapes as carefully as the positive ones: are they clean, intended, and free of awkward slivers?
- **Proximity.** Things close together read as a group. Gaps decide what belongs together; uneven gaps break the
  grouping the design intends.
- **Similarity.** Things that look alike are read as the same kind. If two dots look alike, make them identical;
  if they are different on purpose, make the difference obvious.
- **Continuation.** The eye follows lines and curves. Parallel strokes that drift apart, or a curve that changes
  radius without reason, interrupt the flow.
- **Closure.** The eye completes incomplete shapes. A mark can leave parts out if what remains implies them
  clearly.
- **Rhythm.** Repeated widths and spaces create rhythm (for example stroke 40, gap 40). Breaking the rhythm once
  draws attention to that spot; do it only where you want attention.

## 5. Balance and symmetry

- A symmetric mark must be exactly symmetric; a near miss looks like a mistake. Rebuild one half from the other.
- An asymmetric mark needs balance: the visual weight should feel settled, not falling.
- The optical centre of a frame is slightly above the geometric centre. A mark placed exactly in the middle of a
  square often looks low; nudge it up a little in avatars and app icons.
- Shapes with two stacked parts (S, B, 8, 3, many monograms) look top-heavy if both parts are the same size. Type
  designers make the upper part slightly smaller or narrower. In a strictly geometric mark this is a choice; note
  it, do not demand it.
- Weight at the bottom feels stable; weight at the top feels dynamic or unstable. Neither is wrong; it must match
  the personality.

## 6. Scale

- Check the mark at 16, 24, 32, 48, 64 and 128 px (`08-sizes.png`). Gaps narrower than one pixel fill in and
  strokes under one pixel fade.
- Rule of thumb: a gap or stroke should be at least about 1/16 of the mark's height to survive at 16 px.
- If the full mark fails small, design a **simplified version** for favicons, app icons and avatars: fewer
  elements, thicker strokes, wider gaps, the same idea. Large brands do this routinely; it is not a weakness.
- Check the large end too: at billboard size, small flaws (a slightly flat curve, an uneven joint) become visible.
- Define a **minimum size** for print and screen, below which only the simplified version is used.

## 7. Type in a logo

- **Weight match.** The name's stroke weight should relate to the mark's: similar, or clearly different.
- **Spacing.** Letters in a logo are spaced by eye, not by the font's default. Check pairs such as AV, LT, To, and
  round letters next to straight ones; adjust until the gaps look even.
- **Case and tracking.** All capitals usually need extra letter spacing (about 5 to 15% of the font size) to read
  well; lowercase rarely does.
- **Legibility.** Test the name at the smallest size it will be used. Thin hairlines and tight counters fail first.
- **Licence.** The font's licence must allow use in a logo. Many free fonts (SIL Open Font License) allow it;
  some commercial ones need a specific licence. Outline the name in the master files so it never depends on an
  installed font.

## 8. The logo system

A professional logo is a small system, not one file.

- **Versions:** primary lockup (mark and name), stacked lockup, mark only, simplified small mark, one-colour
  positive, one-colour reversed.
- **Clear space:** the empty margin kept around the logo, usually defined by a part of the mark (for example
  the height of a letter or the diameter of a ring), so it scales with the logo.
- **Minimum size:** for each version, in pixels and in millimetres.
- **Backgrounds:** which version goes on light, dark, mid tone and photographic backgrounds.
- **Misuse:** what never to do (stretch, recolour, add effects, rotate, crowd).

## 9. Originality and legal basics

You cannot clear a logo; only a trademark search and a lawyer can. What you can do:

- **Look for resemblance.** Ask the owner whether the mark resembles any logo in their market. A simple geometric
  mark (a circle, a triangle, a letter in a box) is likely to resemble existing marks.
- **Recommend a search.** Before investing in a mark, the owner should run a trademark search in the relevant
  classes and countries, ideally with a professional.
- **Generated artwork.** Copyright offices in several countries, including the United States, have said that
  material generated by AI without enough human authorship cannot be registered for copyright. A mark that a
  person has substantially redrawn and refined is on firmer ground. Trademark protection comes from use and
  registration rather than authorship, but a generated mark can also echo existing marks it was trained on. Say
  this plainly when a mark came from an image generator, and suggest legal advice for anything important.
- **Assets and fonts.** Check licences of any font, icon or stock element in the mark.

## 10. Process for a new mark

1. **Brief.** Business, audience, competitors, three personality words, where the logo will be used, must-haves
   and must-avoids.
2. **Research.** Look at the category to see what is common (to avoid) and what is missing (to own).
3. **Ideas.** Write ten or more ideas as short descriptions before drawing any: a letterform, a symbol, an
   abstract shape, a combination. Pick two or three with the strongest single idea.
4. **Construction.** Build each on a grid (`construction.md`): choose a module, a stroke weight, a gap, an angle
   system and a small set of radii. Draw it as exact geometry, in code or in a design tool with snapping.
5. **Check.** Run `review.py`; fix construction faults; run the visual tests in sections 3 and 6.
6. **Optical pass.** Apply the corrections in `construction.md`, section Optical corrections, by eye, after the
   geometry is exact.
7. **Colour and type.** Choose colours with `colour.md`; set the name and space it by eye.
8. **Present.** Two or three directions, each with its idea in one sentence, shown in context (website header,
   avatar, business card, favicon), in colour and one colour.
9. **Refine and deliver** the system in section 8.

## 11. Severity: fix, consider, keep

Sort every point into one of three groups; owners act on clear priorities.

- **Fix:** an objective fault that weakens the mark at any size or makes it look unfinished: near-equal weights,
  uneven gaps between parallel strokes, circles that should match but do not, a near miss in symmetry or
  alignment, a colour that disappears on a background the brand uses.
- **Consider:** a judgment that would improve it, where the owner may reasonably decide otherwise: an optical
  correction, a simplified small version, a colour variant, a balance adjustment.
- **Keep:** what works and should not be lost in the fixes. Always name at least one strength.
